# `(?=…)` and `(?!…)` — assert what follows, consume nothing

**One line:** A lookahead succeeds if its sub-pattern would match at the current position and then leaves the position where it was — positive `(?=…)`, negative `(?!…)` — in every engine here except Go and Rust's `regex`, which have no lookaround at all; and the negative form's most common use is a bug, because "not followed by X" is true one character earlier.

**Level:** reference · one construct, eight engines

## What it means

Zero width: the assertion tests, it does not take. `\d+(?= EUR)` matches `25` in `25 EUR` and the match ends after the `5`; the ` EUR` is checked and left alone. That is what makes lookahead useful in a replacement or an extraction — the context is verified without becoming part of the result.

A lookahead can contain groups, and the groups capture. `(?=(a+))\1` is a lookahead that captures `a+` and a backreference that then consumes it — which is an [atomic group](../atomic_group/README.md) built out of two other features, and the way JavaScript gets one.

### Reading the table

- **Row 1.** `\d+(?= EUR)` gives `25` in six engines. Go and Rust's `regex` refuse the construct entirely.
- **Row 2, the trap.** `\d+(?! EUR)` on `25 EUR` matches **`2`** in all six. The intent was "a number not followed by EUR"; the engine's reading is "a run of digits after which the next text is not ` EUR`", and `2` — followed by `5` — satisfies that. Backtracking found the answer that makes the negative assertion true. Nothing was wrong with the engine.
- **Row 3, the fix.** `\b\d+\b(?! EUR)` — a boundary so the digits cannot stop early — answers `no`, which is the correct answer for this subject. [When the engine has no lookaround](../../03_Engines/when_there_is_no_lookaround/README.md) shows a replacement that is *more* correct than this lookahead.
- **Row 4.** The capturing lookahead, `(?=(a+))\1`, matches `aaa`: the lookahead captured three `a`s, the backreference consumed them.
- **Row 5, lookahead at the start.** `(?=.*\d)\w+` is the password-rule idiom — "must contain a digit" checked before the real match starts — and it works everywhere lookahead exists.

## The trap

Row 2. Negative lookahead after a quantifier is the single most-reported "my regex is wrong" shape in this library's experience, and it is usually the pattern being right and the author's model being wrong. The assertion is evaluated at one position; the quantifier is free to choose that position. Anchor the quantifier's end with a boundary or a possessive/atomic form, or put the assertion *before* the quantifier where its meaning is fixed.
